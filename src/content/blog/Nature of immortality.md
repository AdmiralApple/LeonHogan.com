---
title: "The nature of immortality"
description: "Late game video games."
date: 2026-11-25
tags: ["meta"]
---

Imagine a future where every person has been digitized, making them effectively immortal. Each person exists in their own virtual space, an isolated "bubble", completely independent from every other person's bubble. Bubbles only open to each other if both sides consent.

Each bubble is managed by a hyper-intelligent, near-omniscient AI that has a complete understanding of your digital person. Its goal is to manage your bubble and to follow your instructions.

The first thing a person might do, if they found themselves with infinite time and a perfect simulation, is go on an adventure. For example, let's say you wanted to be a samurai and save your kingdom and whatnot.

In this scenario, it is impossible to actually enjoy the game.

There are two possibilities:

1. **The AI has designed the challenge so that you will succeed.** Since it knows 100% of what you are capable of, it knows exactly which challenges you can overcome. And *you know it knows*, so you know you'll overcome the challenge. The game isn't fun, because you know you will win.
2. **The AI has designed the challenge so that you will NOT succeed.** And that isn't fun either, because if you can't win, what's the point?

One solution to this problem is to suppress your memory. If you don't know you're actually an immortal being, and simply think you're a samurai protecting your kingdom, then you can genuinely enjoy the experience of triumph and gain. You could then have your memory restored at a point of your choosing: the natural death of the samurai, after X elapsed years, or some other trigger set up with the AI.

This solution comes with two problems:

1. **The psychological problem.** Imagine that, after a trillion years or so, you end up in an adventure where you need to suppress your memory again. How long until *that* adventure needs another layer? How would you ever know which layer of suppression you're in? How could you ever verify that you're at the top, and not in a sub-simulation?
2. **The meta problem.** Why ever have different adventures if you can simply forget an adventure and do it again? For example, one of my favorite games is *Outer Wilds*. I always thought, "If I could forget one game and play it again, it'd be *Outer Wilds*." But in a future where you can selectively suppress and restore memories, why stop at once? Why not play *Outer Wilds* over and over for eternity, since each time is just as fun as the first? Boredom requires memory.

This brings us to yet another problem: why not skip the middleman?

In a fully digital existence, every feeling and experience can be observed and simulated. If having an adventure triggers a certain neurological response, then skip the adventure and have the AI write a script to induce that response. Forever.

It's a grim future, but it makes you realize that most of the value we place on everything depends on the idea that things take time, and that we will eventually die.

Which leaves us with two possible endings:

1. We become immortal, never dying, and eventually experience every possible experience.
2. We die, and become unable to experience anything at all.
